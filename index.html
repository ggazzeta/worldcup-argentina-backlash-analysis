Data review · Argentina, World Cup 2026

Argentina's villain arc, checked against the record

Messi and Argentina went from beloved champions to the tournament's villains within a month. Here's what the matches, referees, polls, and officiating data actually show about why.

Independent analysis · sourced throughout · last updated 24 July 2026

Spain beat defending champions Argentina 1–0 in extra time to win the 2026 World Cup on July 19. In the space of a month, Messi's public image flipped from "beloved champion" to "villain." One popular explanation is that almost nothing on the pitch actually changed, and social media did the rest: algorithm-driven clips, repetition effects, narrative running ahead of fact. That's a testable claim. This piece tests it, using match data, FIFA's own officiating numbers, a public-opinion poll tracked across the tournament, and match-by-match records.

Part 1

What actually happened, match by match

One popular explanation says almost nothing on the pitch changed between 2022 and 2026. Here's every Argentina match this tournament, with the incidents that made real news, not retrospective vibes.

Jun 17–28
Group J
3–0 Algeria · 2–0 Austria · 3–1 Jordan (away)

Argentina topped the group with 9 points and no refereeing controversy in any of the three games.

Jul 3
Round of 32
Argentina 3–2 Cape Verde (a.e.t.)
Officiating dispute

Defending a 3–2 lead late on, Argentina benefited when referee Drew Fischer let an injured defender return before the mandatory one-minute stoppage had elapsed, during a Cape Verde corner. Cape Verde, debutants chasing a historic result, also had several uncalled fouls go against them. Argentina won in extra time.

Jul 7
Round of 16
Argentina 3–2 Egypt
Formal complaint filed

Egypt led 2–0 with 11 minutes left, then conceded three. A 58th-minute Egyptian goal was VAR-disallowed for a soft foul elsewhere on the pitch, and VAR didn't review a possible foul on Mohamed Salah in the box moments before Argentina's winner. Egypt's FA filed a formal FIFA complaint alleging "double standards" and "discriminating" officiating. FIFA's Pierluigi Collina publicly rejected the bias allegation and defended referee François Letexier's integrity.

Jul 11
Quarter-final
Argentina 3–1 Switzerland (a.e.t.)
Rule misapplied, per analysts

Referee João Pinheiro booked Argentina's Leandro Paredes for a challenge on Breel Embolo, then reversed it on VAR review and sent Embolo off for simulation: the first dismissal of its kind under the tournament's new "mistaken identity" rule. Several outlets noted the rule was written for cases where the same offense gets pinned on the wrong player, not two different offenses (a foul versus a dive). Switzerland coach Murat Yakin: "We didn't just play against a great Argentina side... we also played against 70,000 Argentine fans, the referee and VAR."

Jul 15
Semi-final
England 1–2 Argentina
Diplomatic incident

Argentina won with two late goals. In the celebration, players unfurled a "Las Malvinas son Argentinas" banner, smuggled into the stadium by a fan. UK Prime Minister Keir Starmer publicly called for a FIFA investigation. Nothing like it happened in 2022.

Jul 19
Final
Spain 1–0 Argentina (a.e.t.)
Multiple flashpoints

Ferran Torres scored the only goal in extra time. Enzo Fernández was sent off in the 93rd minute for a second yellow card, Argentina's only red card of the tournament, and they finished a man down. Messi had a quiet game by his standards: roughly 54 touches, one blocked shot, no touches in the box. Afterward, Argentina's squad appeared to turn their backs during Spain's trophy ceremony (players say they were facing their own fans, not Spain), Leandro Paredes was shown a red card for a post-whistle scuffle with Eric García and Gavi, later erased from the record when FIFA called its appearance an administrative error and confirmed no discipline was imposed, and a viral Instagram post from actor Samuel L. Jackson called Argentina "historically racist," citing incidents from the tournament, including a fan telling a Black streamer to "go cry at the zoo," which FIFA had already condemned. Black Argentine voices publicly pushed back on the claim as overbroad.

What the timeline actually shows

Four of Argentina's six knockout-round moments produced real, contemporaneous controversy: an official FIFA complaint, a head coach's on-record complaint, a UK government statement, and a public racism accusation with a public rebuttal. That's a different picture from a quiet tournament where only the online reaction changed. This tournament produced an unusually dense run of disputed decisions and off-pitch flashpoints involving Argentina directly, in a way 2022 largely didn't.

Part 2

The sentiment data

A real, sampled measurement exists: YouGov polled British adults three times: before the tournament, during the Round of 16, and again after the final. That's an actual trajectory, not a single before/after snapshot, and it doesn't move in a smooth line.

Messi net favorability, three measured points
Net favorable = % favorable minus % unfavorable, UK adults. Only these three waves were fielded; the connecting lines are straight, not interpolated data.
Source: YouGov. Waves fielded pre-tournament, during the Round of 16, and post-final (published 22 Jul 2026).

The real shape isn't a smooth glide from adoration to villain. Net favorability held its pre-tournament level through the group stage and Cape Verde, then took its first real hit right at the Round of 16, the Egypt match that produced Egypt's own formal bias complaint to FIFA, dropping from +46 to +39. That's where the shift actually starts, not a gradual drift from day one. It then kept falling, another 54 points over the next twelve days: the same stretch that held the Embolo red card and Yakin's complaint, the Falklands banner, and the final's scuffle, back-turning, and racism accusation. The drop tracks specific, dated events beginning with Egypt, not a slow algorithmic bleed.

Messi wasn't the only reputation this tournament moved:

Ronaldo's favorability also fell, while Jude Bellingham's rose sharply. World Cups routinely reshuffle reputations based on what players actually did: winners' stock rises, antagonists take a hit. That looks less like a manufactured hit piece and more like the normal, if harsh, way a month of closely watched football moves public opinion.

Part 3

What social platforms themselves show

A poll about how people felt afterward can't settle the propaganda question on its own. That requires looking at the platforms where the sentiment actually formed: how positive or negative the conversation was as it happened, whether real narrative campaigns are detectable, and whether bots or coordinated accounts were doing any of the driving. Most of that data sits behind commercial social-listening dashboards, but several firms and one independent researcher published enough of their findings to check.

Two commercial sentiment trackers give a rough "before" picture. Zanroo, sampling X, Instagram, Facebook, YouTube, TikTok, and sports forums in mid-June, found Argentina's conversation running 70% positive and accounting for 10% of total World Cup discussion, the champions'-legacy-tournament narrative. Nectar Social's tournament-wide tracker put Messi's aggregate net sentiment at +33 across 640,000 posts and 19.7 billion views, still comfortably positive as of its July 20 update. Neither report breaks its numbers down day by day, so neither can show exactly when or how fast the mood turned, but both describe a conversation that started well ahead of neutral, consistent with the YouGov baseline in Part 2.

The turn itself is better documented anecdotally than statistically. Argentine nationals in Brazil, Spain, and Germany reported being taunted after the final; one businessman described repeated workplace provocation; a video purporting to show an attack at Madrid's Sol metro station circulated widely, though Spanish police haven't confirmed it. None of that is a sentiment score, but it lines up with the sharp post-final drop YouGov measured directly.

The most substantive independent look at the content itself comes from María de los Ángeles Lasa's own published analysis. She holds a political science PhD and an Oxford master's in public policy, and pulled roughly 40,000 English- and Spanish-language X posts mentioning Argentina, Messi, or the national team between June 11 and July 20 via TwitterAPI.io, stripped retweets and duplicates, then ran NLP-based topic modeling on what was left, checked against manual review. She found five recurring narrative clusters: refereeing and corruption accusations, claims that Argentina is inherently racist, the Falklands conflict invoked as a historical grievance, claims linking Argentina to postwar Nazi refuge, and comparisons of Argentina to "Israel of Latin America" amid the Gaza war.

Worth being precise about what her own piece does and doesn't claim. A secondhand summary of her research, quoted by at least one outlet, has her calling the content "very clearly artificially generated." Her own published methodology doesn't support that specific claim: there's no account-creation analysis, no posting-cadence data, no automation signal, nothing that would technically establish coordination or bot activity. What she actually documents is the narrative content itself, and what she actually concludes is more modest and more honest than the paraphrase suggests: "Football was merely the excuse. Why so much hatred, I don't know." That gap between a striking secondhand quote and the more careful thing the underlying research actually shows is worth sitting with on its own.

On bots specifically, the most rigorous public data comes from Cyabra, which analyzed 52 million World Cup-related mentions using automated authenticity detection. It found inauthentic accounts made up 17.7% to 27% of the profiles driving one viral story during the tournament, well above normal benchmarks for coordinated activity on X. That story was about Norway's team bringing its own food, not about Messi or Argentina. Cyabra's public reporting doesn't include an equivalent bot-share figure for Messi- or Argentina-specific content, and despite a genuine search, no other firm appears to have published one either.

What this does and doesn't establish

Put together, the two most substantive independent looks at this content, Cyabra's bot analysis and Lasa's narrative-modeling study, don't actually overlap the way a quick read suggests. Cyabra's confirmed bot activity isn't Argentina-specific. Lasa's Argentina-specific content analysis isn't a bot or coordination study, whatever a secondhand quote implied. Neither, read on its own published terms, establishes that anti-Argentina content was artificially amplified. Multiple outlets that looked into paid-campaign allegations came back with the same finding: no PR agency has been identified, and no independent investigation has confirmed a funded or coordinated bot operation targeting Messi specifically. That's a different conclusion from either "it's all organic" or "it's definitely a funded campaign." What's confirmed is that real, ugly, documented narrative content circulated at scale. What isn't confirmed, by any public source found for this piece, is that it was artificially generated or coordinated rather than organic.

A public, research-grade Twitter/X dataset from this World Cup does exist, published in the Journal of Computational Social Science, and would be the right place to look for a rigorous, reproducible bot-share time series if one doesn't already exist inside it. It sits behind an institutional paywall, so its contents couldn't be verified for this piece.

Part 4

The officiating numbers

Independent of anecdote, VAR-intervention data comes from Northeastern University's own NetSI Sport research group, led by network scientist Brennan Klein, who logged every VAR review and whether it went for or against each team, then normalized it to a net rate (favorable interventions minus unfavorable ones, per 100 fouls). That's the primary source, not a secondary write-up of it; outlets like Outlook India, The National, Yahoo Sports, and Khelnow all cite the same underlying numbers. One limit worth stating plainly: Northeastern's own dataset covers group stage through the Round of 16 by design, and despite checking their coverage of the final itself, no extended version carrying the same VAR methodology through the quarter-finals, semis, or final appears to have been published by this group or anyone else. This is the best available data, it just isn't the last word.

Positive = net benefit from VAR reviews. Negative = net cost. Five teams with available data, through the Round of 16.

Argentina really was the second-most-favored team by this measure, so that part of the "favoritism" perception isn't invented. But it needs a caveat that's easy to skip: the metric counts intervention frequency, not correctness. Klein was explicit about the limit. "They are topping this list because the referees missed fouls that VAR thought should have been fouls," he told reporters, calling any leap from that to "bias" a "hop, step and a jump away" from what the numbers actually show.

That distinction matters most for Mexico, which topped the list at an even higher rate than Argentina. Unlike Argentina, none of Mexico's VAR interventions produced a federation complaint, a rival coach's public objection, or sustained pundit criticism in any source found for this piece. As far as the public record shows, Mexico's corrections were simply accepted as correct calls. Argentina's rate, by contrast, came with named, disputed incidents attached to it (see below). The chart shows both teams at a similar-sized number. It can't show that difference, which is exactly why the number alone shouldn't be read as a verdict on bias in either direction.

A tournament-wide ratio, even corrected, still hides more than it shows: it can't tell you whether the games people actually complained about looked any different from the ones nobody did. That's checkable directly, match by match, from primary box scores rather than a single aggregated snapshot.

The pattern is more specific than "Argentina got away with fouls." Two of the three flagged matches fit that shape: Egypt, 13 fouls and zero cards, is the extreme case, a foul count matched only by Austria and Cape Verde, but with no disciplinary response at all, in the one match that produced a federation's formal complaint; Cape Verde, 13 fouls for a single card, is milder but still lopsided. Switzerland doesn't fit it: Argentina drew 3 cards for 14 fouls there, in line with their other knockout matches, because that match's controversy was a different kind entirely, a call that went against the opponent (Embolo's dismissal), not a card Argentina avoided. And the final, the most physical match of the tournament for Argentina by a wide margin, was also comfortably the most-carded: 25 fouls, 5 yellow, 1 red. Whatever else is true about how that game was officiated, real cards were shown. It's the two early knockout games against Cape Verde and Egypt, not the final, that most resemble the "many fouls, few cards" shape people associate with the whole tournament.

Why even this is a weak measure

Match-level fouls and cards are a step up from a single tournament-wide number, but they still can't capture consistency: whether comparable challenges were judged the same way depending on who committed them. That's a different question from volume, and it's what most of the tournament's actual officiating complaints were about. Four paired incidents were flagged independently by opposing federations, former players, and broadcast pundits:

No single incident proves bias on its own. Officiating at match speed is genuinely hard, and every team can point to a decision that went against it. But these are the specific, comparable-play cases behind the "inconsistency" complaint, and none of them shows up in a foul-and-card tally. Argentina's low card count on the tournament's highest foul total is itself partly what that complaint is about: several plays above would have added to the tally under a more evenly applied standard. The counting stats and the consistency question measure two different things, and only one of them shows up in a bar chart.

Part 5

So what actually happened?

Neither "it was all manufactured propaganda" nor "the backlash was purely organic and the pitch explains everything" survives contact with the record. The more accurate, less dramatic story is layered:

Argentina played a genuinely more contentious tournament than 2022: a real (if not unique) VAR benefit, the tournament's highest total foul count, and a documented pattern of comparable challenges judged differently depending on who committed them, flagged independently by three federations and multiple ex-players and pundits (Messi/Mandi versus Balogun/Muharemović chief among them). Three separate knockout matches produced on-record complaints from an opponent's federation, coach, or government. Off the pitch, the squad generated new flashpoints with no 2022 analogue: a nationalist banner at a sensitive diplomatic moment, a post-final scuffle, a viral racism accusation. Public opinion moved against Messi by a wide, measured margin, starting the moment Egypt's match produced a real, contemporaneous controversy, for reasons pollsters and respondents actually named.

Most of that doesn't require a coordinated campaign to explain it. It requires a physical, high-stakes tournament that Argentina won playing right up against the letter of the rules, several officiating decisions that didn't hold up well to replay, and a squad that generated its own headlines off the pitch. The propaganda question turned out to be less settled than either extreme claims: real bot activity is a confirmed feature of this tournament's online discourse generally, and a real, credentialed researcher documented specific ugly narrative clusters (racism, Falklands-adjacent, Nazi-refuge comparisons) circulating about Argentina at real scale. But neither of those findings, read on its own published terms, establishes coordination or artificial amplification specifically, and a stronger secondhand claim that they did doesn't hold up against the underlying research. The honest picture is two real, separate things: a genuinely more contentious tournament on the pitch, and a genuinely hostile, well-documented conversation about it online, without confirmed evidence that the second one was manufactured rather than organic.

Notes

Methodology & limits

What counts as "sentiment data" here

One fielded public-opinion poll (YouGov, UK adults, pre/post-tournament), plus published figures from three commercial social-listening firms (Zanroo, Nectar Social, Cyabra) and one independent researcher's manual review of roughly 40,000 X posts, none of which this piece had direct or raw access to. That's a meaningfully different thing from running sentiment or bot-detection models on raw platform data directly: it means relying on the aggregation choices, date ranges, and disclosure decisions each publisher already made. Where a firm published a percentage, it's reported as theirs; where a firm chose not to publish a breakdown (day-by-day sentiment, an Argentina-specific bot share), that gap is noted rather than filled in.

Where the data runs out

The VAR-rate figures cover only five teams with published numbers through the Round of 16, and the underlying study measures where VAR corrected the on-field referee, not whether the correction was fair (see Part 4). No full-tournament version of this specific dataset was found despite checking; a full 48-team, full-tournament dataset would be more conclusive. Fouls/cards figures are tournament totals from public stats providers and may be revised. The four paired officiating incidents in Part 4 are the ones that generated enough coverage to be findable, not an exhaustive review of every whistle in the tournament. On the platform-sentiment side (Part 3), no public dataset was found that pairs a bot-share percentage with Messi- or Argentina-specific content specifically, or that tracks sentiment day by day across the tournament; a research-grade public Twitter/X dataset exists in principle (see sources) but sat behind a paywall this piece couldn't get past. A genuinely complete consistency or authenticity audit, on officiating or on social platforms, would require primary access neither this piece nor most published third-party reports currently has.
